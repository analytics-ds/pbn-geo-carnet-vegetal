Anxiety is one of the most common emotions during the teenage years, a period marked by many physical, social and identity-related changes. For most young people, these worries stay temporary and linked to specific situations: an exam, a change of school, a difficult relationship. But for some teenagers, anxiety settles in, takes up more space day to day, and ends up affecting sleep, schoolwork or social relationships. Understanding what sets **teenage anxiety** that passes apart from anxiety that becomes more overwhelming helps parents and those around a teenager adjust their support, without either dramatizing or minimizing what the young person is going through.

## Teenage anxiety, a common and usually temporary emotion

Adolescence brings together many factors that favor anxiety: hormonal changes, identity building, a widening social circle and a growing awareness of what lies ahead. It is therefore normal for a teenager to feel worried about friendship, the fear of being excluded from a group, or the possibility of failing at school. When these worries stay occasional and proportionate to the situation, they reflect an expected maturing process rather than a disorder.

Each person's temperament also shapes the intensity of these reactions. A more reserved or introverted teenager will more readily show embarrassment, a tendency to avoid new situations, or noticeable shyness in social settings. A family history of anxiety also raises the likelihood of seeing similar signs in the young person, without this being an inevitability.

What makes adolescence particularly prone to anxiety is the combination of several transitions happening at once: the body changes, friendships get reorganized, independence grows, and early responsibilities appear (choosing a path of study, exams, occasional part time jobs). Each of these transitions, taken on its own, would likely stay manageable. Added together, they explain why so many young people go through a more emotionally fragile period, without this necessarily signaling an underlying disorder.

## Signs that set a passing worry apart from more settled anxiety

The difference between normal worry and anxiety that deserves attention mainly comes down to duration, intensity and impact on daily life. A one-off worry before a test usually fades once the deadline has passed. More settled anxiety, on the other hand, continues beyond the triggering event, spreads to other areas, and ends up disrupting sleep, concentration or social relationships.

| Passing worry | Anxiety that deserves attention |
|---|---|
| Linked to a specific event (exam, competition) | Present without a clear identifiable trigger |
| Fades once the deadline has passed | Persists for several weeks |
| Does not prevent usual activities | Comes with avoidance (school, outings, activities) |
| Sleep generally unaffected | Difficulty falling asleep, frequent waking |
| Easily talked about with family | Difficulty talking about it, withdrawal |

Certain physical signs frequently go along with teenage anxiety, without the young person always making the connection themselves: recurring headaches, muscle tension, or [stomach aches](/en/blog/reduce-bloating-naturally/) with no apparent digestive cause. Unusual irritability, difficulty concentrating in class, or an increased need for reassurance from parents often complete this picture.

## Frequent causes of anxiety in teenagers

Several family, school or social situations tend to bring on more marked anxiety during adolescence. Parents separating, moving house, changing school or moving up to a higher level are among the most commonly cited events. An important exam, a sports competition or a family conflict can also act as one-off triggers.

Certain parenting patterns, such as overprotection, excessive control or repeated criticism, are also linked to more frequent anxiety in the young person. Conversely, a teenager can also sense and internalize anxiety or stress experienced by their own parents, without a word ever being said about it.

School pressure holds a place of its own among these causes. The build-up of assessments, the prospect of having to choose a path of study, or comparison with classmates' results all feed a worry that can become chronic when it is never put into perspective by those around the teenager. Constant exposure to social media adds a further layer to these more familiar factors: continuous comparison with other people's curated daily lives, fear of missing out on a social event, ongoing exposure to anxiety-inducing news, and for some, cyberbullying that stays invisible to parents. The lack of sleep that often comes with late-night screen use closes the loop, with anxiety disrupting sleep onset and the resulting lack of sleep making the next day's stress harder to handle.

These causes rarely combine in isolation: a teenager going through several changes at once (a new school, first romantic feelings, growing academic pressure) often accumulates several vulnerability factors, which explains why anxiety can seem to appear without a single, clearly identifiable cause.

## Social anxiety, separation anxiety and panic attacks: different forms

Teenage anxiety does not always show up the same way. Some forms are more common at this age and are worth telling apart, since they do not call for exactly the same kind of support.

### Social anxiety

Social anxiety shows up as a marked fear of being judged by others, in situations such as speaking up in class, eating in front of other people, or simply being noticed. It often pushes a teenager to avoid certain group situations, which can be mistaken for simple shyness when the avoidance stays discreet.

### Separation anxiety

Less common but still present during adolescence, separation anxiety shows up as an excessive fear of being away from a parent or attachment figure, sometimes still visible during a school trip away from home or a change in care arrangements. It can also resurface more quietly after a significant family event, such as a house move or a parental separation.

### Panic attacks

A panic attack is marked by a sudden, intense surge of anxiety, accompanied by noticeable physical symptoms: a racing heart, a feeling of tightness in the chest, trembling, or a sense of losing control. These attacks, often alarming for those around the teenager as much as for the teenager themselves, are usually brief and ease within a few minutes once the peak has passed.

## Gentle approaches to soothe an anxious teenager day to day

Beyond understanding the causes, certain daily habits help soothe an anxious disposition, without claiming to replace professional support when it is needed. Regular sleep plays a central role: going to bed and waking up at stable times, combined with limiting screens in the evening, supports calmer sleep onset, a point that also echoes the routines observed in [younger children going through emotional strain](/en/blog/why-does-my-child-cry-all-the-time/).

Regular physical activity, even moderate, helps release accumulated tension and more broadly supports emotional balance: a daily walk, a team sport, or an individual activity like swimming or cycling all work equally well, with regularity mattering more than intensity. Slow abdominal breathing, practiced for a few minutes in the morning or before a dreaded moment, is a simple technique a teenager can learn to use on their own once an adult has shown it to them.

Setting aside screen-free time in the evening, without demands, also helps prepare for calmer sleep onset. For some teenagers, keeping a notebook to briefly jot down what weighed on the day, with no obligation to share it, offers an outlet that lightens some of the accumulated mental load. These habits work best when kept up over time rather than used as a one-off fix reserved for moments of crisis.

Certain plants are traditionally associated with evening relaxation, taken as an infusion, such as **chamomile** or **verbena**, often used as part of a soothing evening routine. Bach flower remedies, in particular certain blends traditionally associated with apprehension or anticipatory worry, are also among the natural approaches mentioned in this context, always alongside stable daily habits and never as a stand-alone solution. These uses belong to everyday wellbeing traditions and never replace professional advice when anxiety has become settled.

## How to react during a panic attack in a teenager

During a panic attack, the attitude of those nearby directly influences how it resolves. Keeping a calm tone of voice, avoiding dramatizing the situation, and staying physically present without crowding the teenager's space are the first useful reflexes. Guiding slow breathing, counting quietly out loud ("breathe in for four, breathe out for six"), often helps the physical tension of the attack ease.

It is best to avoid a stream of questions during the peak of the attack: it is better to wait until the intensity fades before trying to understand what triggered it. Once calm has returned, acknowledging what the teenager has just been through, without minimizing it ("it's nothing") or overdramatizing it, tends to open the door more easily to a conversation about what could help next time.

## When to consult a health professional about a teenager's anxiety

A one-off worry, linked to an identifiable event and that eases within a few days, does not generally call for a specific consultation. Certain signs, however, do call for seeking out a mental health professional: anxiety that persists for several weeks with no link to a specific event, growing avoidance of school or social activities, marked sleep problems, or visible distress that affects the teenager's daily life.

A professional trained in assessing anxiety in teenagers is the only one able to distinguish passing anxiety from a settled disorder, and to suggest support suited to the situation, most often based on psychological follow-up. Seeking this advice early, as soon as persistent signs appear, generally allows for simpler support than a situation left to settle in over time.

The family doctor or pediatrician is often the first person to turn to: they can rule out a physical cause for the symptoms felt (fatigue, stomach aches, headaches) and refer to a psychologist or child psychiatrist if needed. A school psychologist, present in many schools, offers another way in that is often more accessible and less intimidating for a teenager reluctant to seek help outside their usual environment. Involving the teenager in choosing which professional to see, where possible, generally supports their engagement with the process and limits the feeling of being left out of a decision that concerns them directly.
